Performance Domain 7 of 7

Risk — managing what you can't yet see

Why this matters

Risk management is the most performative discipline in project management. Most risk registers are dead artifacts — populated at kickoff, updated quarterly to satisfy governance, and consulted when someone wants to say "we knew about that." PMBOK 8 reframes risk as continuous, integrated, and equally concerned with opportunities (positive risks) as with threats. A live risk register that drives weekly decisions is worth a hundred dead ones that document what already went wrong. This is the final performance domain — and arguably the one where the gap between PMBOK aspiration and field practice is widest.

Key concepts

01

Risk = uncertainty × impact (positive or negative)

Risks aren't only threats. An opportunity is a positive risk — an uncertain event that could improve the project if it materializes (faster vendor delivery, regulatory window opening, surplus team capacity). PMBOK 8 explicitly includes opportunity management in the Risk domain, because the same identification and response disciplines apply. Most teams under-invest here and miss capturable upside.

02

Identify continuously, not at kickoff only

Risk identification at kickoff is necessary but never sufficient. New risks emerge as the project evolves; old risks resolve or transform; the environment around the project changes. A risk register that hasn't been touched in two months is documenting the past, not managing the future. Set a recurring review — biweekly for active projects — and use it.

03

Response = escalate / avoid / mitigate / transfer / accept (mirror set for opportunities: escalate / exploit / enhance / share / accept)

For threats: escalate (when outside the project's scope or authority), avoid the risk entirely (change the plan), mitigate (reduce probability or impact), transfer (insurance, contracts), or accept (with or without contingency reserve). For opportunities, the mirror set: escalate, exploit (lock it in), enhance (raise its probability), share (partner), or accept. Every risk above the action threshold should have an assigned response, an owner, and a status. "Logged" is not a response.

04

Heat-map the register; act on what's red

The 5×5 probability-impact matrix is a triage tool, not an analysis tool. Plot the risks; the reds get active management; the yellows get response plans; the greens get monitoring. Without this triage, the register becomes a list where critical risks sit next to trivial ones and nothing is prioritized. The diagram above is the format that works.

05

Risk reviews ≠ status reviews

If your weekly status meeting also serves as your risk review, neither one is happening. Status is about what's been done; risk is about what could happen. Mature teams keep them separate — a 30-minute weekly status with a 45-minute biweekly risk session, with different attendance, different artifacts, and different frame of mind. Merging them produces theatre in both.

From the Standard

Integrating risk into governance

PMBOK 8 explicitly states that risk management is integrated into the Governance performance domain — proactive identification and mitigation of risks, and active leveraging of opportunities for added value, are framed as part of how a project is governed rather than as a separate discipline.

In practice this means that risk reviews are part of the governance cadence: steering committees consider top risks and opportunities at every meeting, not just when something has gone wrong. PMI also notes that risk tailoring should reflect organizational risk tolerance — high-tolerance organizations accept more residual risk with less process; low-tolerance organizations (regulated, public-sector) carry heavier risk apparatus by design.

Paraphrased from PMBOK 8 § 2.1 (governance/risk integration) and § 2.7 (Risk Performance Domain).

In your world

The risk that materialized because nobody owned it

A 9-month software integration project had a risk register with 47 entries. Risk #23 read: "Lead architect may rotate to higher-priority project — probability medium, impact high." It had no owner and no response plan beyond "monitor."

In month 6, the lead architect rotated. The team had no documentation of his design decisions, no successor identified, and no cross-training in place. The project slipped six weeks while a replacement got up to speed.

The risk had been on the register the whole time. It was logged but not managed. The lesson PMBOK 8 emphasizes is that the act of logging a risk does not reduce it — only an assigned owner with a real response plan does. "Monitor" without trigger criteria is decoration, not risk management. If the worst-case is high-impact, it should have a name on it.

Technical depth

Qualitative vs quantitative risk analysis: Qualitative analysis (the heat map in the diagram above) ranks risks by estimated probability and impact — fast, useful for prioritization. Quantitative analysis uses numerical models — Expected Monetary Value (EMV = probability × impact in dollars), Monte Carlo simulation (runs thousands of project scenarios to produce probability distributions for cost and schedule), sensitivity analysis (which variable affects the outcome most). Most projects use qualitative; large/regulated projects add quantitative. Note: Risk has 6 processes with 4 in Planning alone — the heaviest planning load of any domain.

PMBOK 8 lists five threat strategies: escalate, avoid, mitigate, transfer, and accept (Guide §5). Hope is not a strategy — though it features prominently in many real risk registers.

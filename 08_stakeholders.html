Performance Domain 5 of 7

Stakeholders — managing the humans who decide if you succeeded

Why this matters

Stakeholders define success. A project that ships on time, on budget, and inside scope, but that key stakeholders refuse to accept, is not successful. PMBOK 8's Stakeholders domain treats engagement as active and bidirectional — you don't just communicate at stakeholders, you build a relationship with them where information, concerns, and decisions flow both ways. The most common failure mode here isn't ignoring stakeholders; it's treating them as a single homogeneous audience that receives the same status update at the same cadence regardless of what they actually need.

Key concepts

01

Stakeholders aren't only sponsors — they're anyone affected

A stakeholder is anyone whose work, role, or interests will be affected by the project — positively, negatively, or both. That includes the sponsor and the steering committee, but also the team members downstream, the customers who'll use the output, the support team who'll inherit it, and adjacent teams whose tools your project touches. Limiting the stakeholder map to the people on the steering committee is the most common stakeholder-management mistake.

02

Power × interest determines engagement strategy

High power + high interest = manage closely (sponsor, lead customer). High power + low interest = keep satisfied (the executive who'll care if it fails but doesn't want weekly updates). Low power + high interest = keep informed (end users, support team). Low power + low interest = monitor (adjacent groups, peripheral vendors). One-size-fits-all communication wastes effort and irritates everyone.

03

Engagement is bidirectional

Sending a status report is not engagement. Engagement is when information flows both ways — you communicate progress and decisions; they communicate concerns, context, and priority shifts. A stakeholder you only broadcast to is a stakeholder who will surprise you with concerns at the worst possible moment.

04

Resistance is data, not noise

When a stakeholder pushes back, the instinct is to manage around them. PMBOK 8's posture: pushback is information. Find out why. Sometimes the resistance reflects a real risk the project plan missed. Sometimes it reflects organizational politics worth understanding. Sometimes it reflects a misunderstanding that's easy to address. None of these are addressed by routing around the stakeholder.

05

Stakeholder maps go stale — refresh them

Stakeholders move on the map as the project evolves. The CFO who was "keep satisfied" at kickoff may become "manage closely" once spend ramps up. The end users who were "monitor" early may become "manage closely" once the change-management phase starts. Refresh the map quarterly at minimum — and any time a major decision or transition is upcoming.

From the Standard

Tailoring stakeholder engagement

PMBOK 8 frames stakeholder engagement as one of the most heavily tailored domains. The engagement approach for a small internal project with 4–5 stakeholders is fundamentally different from the approach for a multi-jurisdictional program with 200+ identified stakeholders. The framework remains the same — identify, analyze, engage, monitor — but the artifacts and cadence scale dramatically.

PMI also emphasizes that stakeholder engagement is an explicit responsibility, not a side effect of good project management. The Guide distinguishes between stakeholders who are informed, consulted, and partnered with, and treats it as a deliberate choice per stakeholder rather than a default broadcast.

In your world

The stakeholder map that surfaced the hidden blocker

A PM running an internal tooling project mapped his stakeholders during the first week. The map had the obvious people — sponsor, dev team, three primary customer groups. He added one less obvious name: the lead of an adjacent team whose existing tool the new one would partially replace.

That lead was placed in "keep informed" — low power over the project, high interest in the outcome. The PM sent her a kickoff briefing and scheduled a monthly check-in.

In month two, she revealed in a check-in that her team had a long-standing integration with the old tool that wasn't documented anywhere. Without that conversation, the new tool would have launched and broken her team's workflow on day one. The map didn't make her important — she was important. The map made her visible. That's the whole job.

7 Stakeholder processes — largest non-governance domain

Technical depth

Communications management is a significant sub-domain. Stakeholders has 7 processes — the most of any non-governance domain — and 3 of them are explicitly about communications: Plan Communications Management, Manage Communications, and Monitor Communications. A communications plan answers: who needs what information, in what format, at what frequency, through what channel. A plan that defaults everyone to the same weekly status email is not a communications plan — it's spam. Note: Stakeholder engagement and communications overlap but are not the same thing. Engagement is about the relationship; communications is about the information flow that supports it.

Performance Domain 3 of 7

Schedule — sequencing work so promises hold

Why this matters

Schedule is the most-shared and most-gamed artifact on any project. Sponsors look at it, stakeholders take dates from it, teams quote against it — and yet most schedules collapse on first contact with reality. PMBOK 8's Schedule domain reframes scheduling as an alignment tool, not a certainty machine. A schedule exists to coordinate humans around when things will happen, surface dependencies that would otherwise bite late, and make slip visible early. If your schedule is being used as a contract for false commitments, you're doing it wrong — and PMBOK 8 says so explicitly.

Key concepts

01

Schedule = sequence + duration + dependencies

Three ingredients. A list of tasks isn't a schedule. Tasks plus dates isn't a schedule. Tasks plus dates plus dependencies is a schedule, because dependencies are what tell you what's actually movable. Get the dependencies wrong and the schedule is fiction regardless of how detailed it looks.

02

Critical path = the longest dependent chain

The critical path is the chain of dependent tasks with no float — slip any of them and the project end-date moves. In the diagram above, Discovery → Design → Build A → Integrate → Launch is critical (38 days). Build B parallels Build A but is shorter, so it has float. The critical path is where your attention belongs; the rest of the schedule is for coordination.

03

Float is both buffer and warning sign

Float (also called slack) is the time a task can slip without affecting the project end date. It's a useful buffer — but float that shrinks rapidly is a warning sign that a non-critical task is becoming critical. If Build B's float drops from 7 days to 2 days over two weeks, something is wrong even if the bar still fits.

04

Adaptive schedules use cadence, not dates

PMBOK 8 explicitly accommodates adaptive scheduling. On an agile project, the "schedule" might be a release backlog with sprint cadence (e.g., release every 4 sprints). The discipline is the same — coordination, dependency visibility, slip detection — but the artifacts differ. Don't force a Gantt onto a discovery-heavy project, and don't force a release backlog onto a regulatory build.

05

Schedule reviews check assumptions, not just progress

A schedule review that only checks "are tasks on track" misses the more important question: are the underlying assumptions still valid? When the integration vendor's quarterly capacity changes, your dependency on them changes, even if today's status is green. PMBOK 8 frames schedule monitoring as continuous reassessment of the model, not just measurement against it.

From the Standard

How PMBOK 8 frames schedule for adaptive projects

PMBOK 8 explicitly acknowledges that traditional Gantt scheduling does not fit every project. For adaptive projects, the schedule may take the form of a product backlog with iteration cadence, a release plan with milestone dates rather than task-level dates, or a rolling-wave plan where near-term work is detailed and long-term work is sketched.

The underlying intent is unchanged across approaches: stakeholders need to know when things will happen, the team needs to know what comes next, and slip needs to be visible early. The form of the schedule should match the project context — predictive projects get detailed Gantts because work is well-defined; adaptive projects get cadence-based plans because work emerges.

In your world

The schedule that survived contact with reality

A senior PM on a 14-month integration project had a habit her sponsor initially found odd: every Monday, she ran a "float check" — a quick scan of every non-critical task to see how much float it had used since the previous Monday.

In month 4, she noticed that a task originally scheduled with 18 days of float had dropped to 3 days of float in two weeks, without anyone flagging it. Digging in, she found that an upstream design decision had slipped quietly, and three non-critical tasks were all going to become critical within a sprint.

She raised it before the slip propagated, the team reordered two work streams, and the critical path stayed intact. Cost of weekly float check: 20 minutes. Cost avoided: probably a month, plus the credibility hit of telling the sponsor mid-month that the date had slipped.

3 Schedule processes — the leanest domain

Technical depth

Schedule compression: When the date must move in, PMBOK 8 recognizes two techniques. Crashing adds resources to critical-path tasks (costs more, may reduce quality). Fast-tracking runs sequential tasks in parallel (adds risk, may cause rework). Both trade something for time — know what you're trading. Leads and lags: A lead starts the successor early (overlap); a lag delays it (waiting time). Misusing these masks schedule risk. Note: Schedule is the leanest domain with only 3 processes — Plan Schedule Management, Develop Schedule, Monitor and Control Schedule.

What is the critical path?

The critical path has no float. Slip any task on it and the end date moves. This is where PM attention belongs; the rest of the schedule is for coordination.

Float shrinking rapidly is a warning that a non-critical task is becoming critical. The status indicator is fine; the float trajectory is the leading indicator that matters.

How does PMBOK 8 frame scheduling for adaptive (agile) projects?

PMBOK 8 explicitly accommodates adaptive scheduling. The form of the schedule changes (backlog + cadence vs detailed Gantt) but the underlying intent — coordination, dependency visibility, slip detection — stays the same.

Which three ingredients together make a real schedule?

A task list isn't a schedule. Tasks plus dates isn't a schedule. Tasks plus dates plus dependencies is a schedule, because dependencies tell you what's actually movable.

What's the main question a schedule review should answer beyond 'are tasks on track'?

Continuous reassessment of the model behind the schedule (vendor capacity, dependency stability, team availability) matters more than measuring against it. Today's green status doesn't mean tomorrow's plan is still valid.

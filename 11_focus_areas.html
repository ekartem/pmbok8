Focus Areas — the "when" axis of project work

Why this matters

Focus Areas are the temporal structure of PMBOK 8. The seven performance domains tell you what to manage; the five focus areas tell you when. If you trained on PMBOK 6, you already know these — they're the five Process Groups under a new name. PMI renamed them because in real projects you rarely run them as crisp sequential phases. You initiate while planning, plan while executing, monitor and control throughout, and close while still executing the tail end. "Focus Areas" reflects this reality better than "Process Groups" did.

Figure 4.1 — The five focus areas. Monitoring & Controlling is the umbrella; the other four overlap rather than handing off cleanly.

Key concepts

01

Focus Areas = v6 Process Groups, renamed

Initiating, Planning, Executing, Monitoring & Controlling, Closing — these are exactly the five Process Groups from PMBOK 6, with no structural change. PMI changed the name from "Process Groups" to "Focus Areas" because the v6 name implied groups of formal processes; the v8 name reflects that today's projects often hit each one through informal practice as much as formal process.

02

They're not phases — they overlap

The diagram is misleading if read as a strict sequence. In practice you initiate a phase while still planning the previous one; you start executing while planning the next chunk; you close work streams while others are still being built. Treating Focus Areas as crisp sequential phases is the most common rookie mistake — and the most common reason gate-heavy waterfall projects feel unnatural to teams.

03

Monitoring & Controlling is continuous

M&C isn't a phase between Executing and Closing — it's an umbrella that runs from project start to project end. From day one you're tracking variance, surfacing issues, and adjusting plans. Treating M&C as something you start "after planning is done" leaves the first few weeks of the project uncovered, which is when most of the avoidable problems get planted.

04

Adaptive projects loop through focus areas per cycle

On a predictive project the focus areas roughly play out once, in sequence. On an adaptive project the team iterates through Planning → Executing → Monitoring repeatedly — each sprint or release contains its own micro-version of the cycle. Same focus areas, different rhythm. PMBOK 8 explicitly accommodates both.

05

Two axes — domains (what) × focus areas (when) — make the matrix

The seven performance domains and the five focus areas form a 7×5 grid. The 40 processes live inside that grid — each process belongs to a specific domain (its topical home) and a specific focus area (its temporal home). Lesson 4.2 plots the full matrix. For now, the mental model: domains are the rows of your work, focus areas are the columns, and processes are the cells.

From the Standard

Why PMI renamed Process Groups to Focus Areas

PMBOK 7 removed the Process Groups entirely as part of its move toward principle-based guidance. The result was that v6-trained practitioners lost a useful temporal scaffold for organizing work. PMBOK 8 brought them back — but renamed.

PMI's explanation, in essence: the original term "Process Groups" implied that each grouping was a bundle of formal processes that ran in sequence. In practice, teams often achieve initiating, planning, executing, monitoring, and closing through informal practices, flexible policies, and tailored ceremonies — not always through five distinct stacks of formal processes. "Focus Areas" captures the temporal grouping without overstating its formality.

Paraphrased from PMBOK 8 Preface and Appendix X5 commentary on the Focus Areas reintroduction.

In your world

Reading the matrix on a real project

A PM running a CRM rollout opened her plan in week 3 of execution. Her domain heat-map showed Risk and Stakeholders were running hot. Her focus-area trace showed Executing was where most attention was concentrated.

Looking at the 7×5 matrix, she noticed that her project had almost no live activity in Monitoring & Controlling for Risk — the risk register hadn't been updated in two weeks. M&C was being applied to Schedule and Scope but not to Risk. The umbrella had a hole in it.

She added a 30-minute biweekly Risk-M&C cell to the standing rhythm. The matrix had told her exactly where the gap was — domains × focus areas wasn't just theoretical structure, it was a diagnostic.

Finance — controlling cost and proving value

Why this matters

Finance is where projects most often lose credibility — and most often deserve to. Over-budget projects damage PMs' careers; under-budget projects that delivered no value damage sponsors'. PMBOK 8's Finance domain treats financial visibility as a value-delivery tool rather than a control mechanism. The shift matters: a PM who reports "actuals are tracking budget" is doing accounting; a PM who reports "forecast-to-complete shows we'll be 15% over and here's why and what I'd change" is doing project finance. The first gets ignored; the second gets believed and gets help.

Key concepts

01

Cost ≠ value (track both)

Cost is what you spent. Value is what the business gets. They are different numbers and one does not predict the other. A project can be 100% on budget and deliver 0% of intended value; another can be 30% over budget and deliver 200% of intended value. Reporting only cost is reporting half the story — and the less interesting half.

02

Budget = baseline; commitments + actuals = reality

The budget is what you planned to spend. Actuals are what you've spent. Commitments are what you've promised to spend (contracts signed, orders placed) but haven't yet paid. Reading only actuals is a lagging indicator — you can be over-committed against your budget months before the actuals show it. Read commitments to see the real position.

03

Forecast-to-complete is the number that matters

Budget-vs-actual tells you where you've been. Forecast-to-complete tells you where you're going — your best estimate of total cost at completion given everything you know now. A monthly forecast update, with a documented variance from budget and a plan to close it (or accept it), is what mature finance looks like under PMBOK 8.

04

Earned value is one technique, not the only

Earned Value Management (EVM) is the classic technique for relating cost, schedule, and scope. It works well on predictive projects with detailed work breakdowns. On adaptive projects it can be misleading — "earned value" of a sprint says nothing about whether the right things got built. Use EVM where it fits; use simpler forecast methods where it doesn't.

05

Tailor financial rigor to risk

A $10M regulatory project needs detailed cost coding, monthly variance reviews, and forecast modeling. A $50K internal pilot needs a budget line and a check-in. Applying the heavy model to the light project wastes effort that could have been spent on actual finance; applying the light model to the heavy project hides the slip until it's a crisis.

From the Standard

Financial monitoring vs financial visibility

PMBOK 8 explicitly distinguishes "financial monitoring" (tracking what has been spent against budget) from "financial visibility" (anticipating what is about to be spent and where surprises are likely to land). Both are part of the Finance domain; both are necessary; they are not the same skill.

PMI also notes that financial regulations and reporting requirements vary widely by industry, geography, and project type. The domain accommodates this by treating the specifics as tailoring decisions — a regulated pharma project will have different financial controls than an internal IT project, and both are correct for their context.

In your world

The PM who avoided a budget overrun by reading commitments

A PM on a hardware-software integration project had a budget of $2.4M and four months remaining. Actuals were at $1.6M (67%) — comfortably on track if you only looked at the actuals number.

But during a routine commitment review, she noticed open purchase orders totalling $760K that hadn't hit actuals yet. Added to actuals, total committed was $2.36M with four months still to spend. The project was going to overrun by ~$200K unless something changed.

She caught it because she read commitments, not actuals. The team renegotiated two contracts, deferred one nice-to-have, and landed at $2.43M — slightly over budget but with sponsor pre-approval, not a surprise. The PM whose career it didn't end was the one who looked at commitments two months before actuals would have surfaced the issue.

Technical depth

Estimation techniques: PMBOK 8 references four common approaches. Analogous uses historical projects as a baseline — fast but rough. Parametric uses statistical relationships (cost per square meter, per function point) — more accurate when the model fits. Bottom-up estimates each work package individually and rolls up — most accurate, most effort. Three-point (optimistic/most likely/pessimistic) accounts for uncertainty and feeds into reserve analysis. Reserve analysis: Contingency reserve covers known risks (owned by PM); management reserve covers unknown unknowns (owned by sponsor). Confusing the two is a common budget-reporting error.
